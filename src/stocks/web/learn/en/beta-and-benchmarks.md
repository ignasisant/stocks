"I made 7 %" means nothing until you compare it with something. These tools tell you whether your result was skill, the market, or just extra risk.

## The idea
The **benchmark** is the yardstick: a realistic alternative you could have bought instead of your portfolio. Pick one that resembles what you hold. A portfolio of tech stocks is compared with the Nasdaq 100, not with the Ibex. And compare in your own currency, with your own deposits on the same dates.

**Beta** measures how much your portfolio moves relative to the index. Beta 1 moves the same. **Beta 1.3** moves 30 % more: if the index rises 10 %, you tend to rise 13 %, and if it falls 10 %, you tend to fall 13 %. Beta 0.7 cushions. Beta is measured from past data and changes.

**Correlation** runs from −1 to +1 and shows whether two assets move together. The lower it is between your holdings, the more it helps to **diversify**: some rise while others fall. Ten highly correlated stocks behave like a single one.

**Alpha** is what you earn above what your beta explains. And the **Sharpe ratio** is the return above the risk-free rate, divided by volatility: how much you earn per unit of risk.

## An example
Your portfolio rises 14 % in a year with beta 1.3. The index rose 10 %.

- Expected from beta: 1.3 × 10 % = 13 %.
- Approximate alpha: 14 % − 13 % = **+1 %**.

You did not do much better: you took 30 % more risk and got 30 % more upside, plus a little extra.

Sharpe: return 9 %, risk-free 3 %, volatility 15 % → (9 − 3) / 15 = **0.4**.

## The common mistake
Comparing yourself with the wrong index, or crediting your skill when it was just high beta in a rising market. Making 14 % in a year when the market rises 12 % is not the same as making it when the market falls.

Also: believing that many positions means diversification. If they are all US tech, correlation is high and diversification is low.
