You buy a US stock and it rises. But your money is in euros, and the exchange rate plays a part too. You can be right about the company and still earn less than you expected.

## The idea
Your **base currency** is the one you think and spend in: normally the euro. If you buy something in another currency, your result depends on two things: what the asset does, and what that currency does against yours. The second factor is **currency risk**.

There are two ways to reduce it:

- **Hedged ETFs**: they offset the exchange-rate effect with financial contracts. The cost usually comes from the interest-rate difference between the two currencies, plus a somewhat higher fee. And if the dollar rises, you also give up that gain.
- **Companies with global revenue**: a dollar-listed company selling around the world already has part of its business in other currencies. The cover is partial and indirect, not automatic.

## An example
You invest €1,000 when €1 = $1.10. You buy $1,100 of a stock.

The stock rises 10 % and is worth $1,210. But the dollar falls 8 % against the euro: each dollar is now worth 8 % less in euros.

- In dollars: $1,100 → $1,210 (+10 %).
- In euros: 1,210 / 1.10 = €1,100 at the original rate, and × 0.92 for the dollar's fall = **€1,012**.
- Return in euros: **+1.2 %**, not +10 %.

Had the dollar risen 8 %, the result would have been 1,100 × 1.08 = €1,188: +18.8 %. The exchange rate cuts both ways.

## The common mistake
Looking only at the return in the asset's currency. What counts is the one in your base currency. Also check which currency each holding is listed in: a European stock may be listed in euros and sell mostly in dollars, and a US one may earn revenue worldwide.

The opposite extreme: hedging everything without thinking. Hedging costs money and does not always pay off, especially over many years, where currencies tend to weigh less in the result.
