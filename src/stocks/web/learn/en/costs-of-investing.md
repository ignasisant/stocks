Costs do not show up as a loss, but they are always deducted. One percentage point a year looks small, and over 20 years it weighs a lot.

## The idea
These are the most common costs:

- **Commission**: what the broker charges for each trade. It can be flat or a percentage.
- **Spread**: the gap between buying and selling. It appears on no statement, but you pay it.
- **Currency conversion**: if you buy a dollar stock with euros, someone converts. Many brokers advertise "zero commission" and charge a margin hidden in the exchange rate. Compare the rate you get with the real market rate.
- **Custody**: some brokers charge a fee for holding your securities, or for holding them in certain markets.
- **Fund costs**: the TER of a fund or ETF, taken from its value with no invoice.

Each one is small on its own. Together, and repeated every year, they matter.

## An example
You invest €10,000 for 20 years with compound interest. Two scenarios:

- No extra cost, 7 % a year return: €10,000 × 1.07²⁰ ≈ €10,000 × 3.87 = **€38,697**.
- With 1 % a year in costs, 6 % net return: €10,000 × 1.06²⁰ ≈ €10,000 × 3.21 = **€32,071**.

The difference is €38,697 − €32,071 = **€6,626**, about 17 % of the final result. And only one point changed.

What hurts most is that the cost is also paid on accumulated gains: every year you lose that 1 % of a bigger capital.

## The common mistake
Looking only at the per-trade commission and forgetting the rest. A "free" broker with an expensive currency conversion can end up worse than one with an explicit commission.

Another mistake is trading too much. Every buy and sell pays spread and commission, however small.

What helps: check how much you pay in total per year, not trade by trade, and compare it with your portfolio.
