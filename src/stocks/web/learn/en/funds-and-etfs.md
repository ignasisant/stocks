Buying a stock is a bet on one company. Buying a fund is buying a basket of many at once. There are several kinds of basket, with very different costs.

## The idea
An **index** is a list of companies that measures a market, such as the IBEX 35 or the S&P 500. You do not buy it directly: it is a yardstick.

- **Mutual fund**: a manager pools money from many investors and invests it. It is bought and sold at the day's closing price, through the fund company.
- **Index fund**: a fund that copies an index without trying to beat it. It is usually cheap.
- **ETF**: a fund, normally index-tracking, that trades on an exchange like a stock. You buy and sell it any time during the session.

The yearly cost is shown as the **TER** (total expense ratio). It is taken out of the fund's value every day, with no bill to see.

In Europe, most ETFs and funds are sold under the **UCITS** rules, which set diversification and protection standards. Many US ETFs lack the key information document (KID) that the PRIIPs regulation requires for EU retail investors, so European brokers do not let you buy them. The usual alternative is a UCITS ETF that tracks the same index.

Some ETFs are **accumulating** (they reinvest dividends inside the fund) and others **distributing** (they pay them out to you).

## An example
You put €10,000 in an index fund with a 0.20 % TER and in an actively managed fund with a 1.50 % TER. Assuming the same gross return:

- Index fund: €10,000 × 0.20 % = **€20 a year**.
- Active fund: €10,000 × 1.50 % = **€150 a year**.

That is a €130 gap every year, and it grows as the capital grows.

## The common mistake
Believing "ETF" means "diversified" or "safe". An ETF on a single theme or country can be very risky. What matters is which index it copies, not the label.

Accumulation is also confused with "paying no tax". An accumulating ETF pays you no dividends, but the gain is still taxed when you sell it. Always check the TER, the index and the dividend policy before buying.
