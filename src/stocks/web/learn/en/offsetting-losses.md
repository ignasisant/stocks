A realised loss is not only a bad moment: it can reduce the tax you pay on your gains. There are rules about how and when.

## The idea
Inside the savings base, the year's capital gains and losses are **netted against each other**. If you make €5,000 on one sale and lose €3,000 on another, you are taxed on €2,000.

If the year's balance ends **negative**, you can apply it against investment income (dividends and interest) up to **25 %** of that income. Whatever is left is **carried forward four years** to offset future gains.

## An example
Ana ends the year with a capital-gains balance of −€4,000 and has received €1,200 in dividends and interest.

- Cross-offset: 25 % of €1,200 = **€300**. She is taxed on €900 instead of €1,200.
- Loss left over: 4,000 − 300 = **€3,700**, which she can use over the next four years.

## Harvesting losses
**Tax-loss harvesting** means deliberately selling a position that is at a loss, to realise the loss and use it.

Example: you have realised €10,000 of gains this year and hold another position with −€2,000 unrealised. If you sell it, you are taxed on €8,000 instead of €10,000. At 19 % you save €380.

But note: it is a **deferral**, not free money. If you buy the same stock back, your cost falls and the future gain will be larger.

## Interaction with the two-month rule
If you buy back the same security within two months before or after the sale, the loss is deferred and the harvest does not work that year. There are two ways to handle it:

- Wait until the two months pass before buying back.
- Meanwhile, invest in something similar but not identical (for example, a fund from another provider tracking a similar index).

## The common mistake
Selling to save tax and abandoning a good investment. The tax should not drive your strategy.

And another: forgetting that the carry-forward expires after four years. Note which year each pending loss comes from. If your case mixes several years or products, consult an adviser.
