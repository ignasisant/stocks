Your portfolio can be up 8 % and still have lost you money. That is not a contradiction: there are two ways to measure a return, and each answers a different question.

## The idea
The **time-weighted return (TWR)** measures how well your investments did, regardless of when you put money in or took it out. It chains each period's return as if the capital never changed. It answers "did I pick well?" and is the one to compare with an index.

The **internal rate of return (IRR)** measures what *your money* earned, taking into account how much was invested at each moment. A euro that arrives just before a fall weighs more than one that was there for the rise. It answers "how much did I make?".

## An example
Ana invests €1,000. In the first year the portfolio rises 20 % to €1,200. Encouraged, she adds another €10,000. In the second year it falls 10 % and ends at €10,080.

- **TWR**: 1.20 × 0.90 − 1 = **+8 %** over two years. Her holdings did well overall.
- **IRR**: she put in €11,000 and has €10,080. Her money lost about **−7.7 % a year**, because almost all of it arrived just before the fall.

Both figures are right. The gap between them is the effect of *when* she moved money.

## The common mistake
Comparing the IRR with an index. An index makes no deposits or withdrawals, so the fair comparison is TWR against the index. The IRR is for something else: telling whether your decisions to add or withdraw helped or hurt.

Another trap: annualising very short periods. A +5 % month "annualised" is almost +80 %, a figure that says nothing useful.
