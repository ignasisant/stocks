Looking only at how far a share's price has risen tells half the story. What really happened to you is the total return, and to compare it with others you need to express it per year and strip out inflation.

## The idea
The **total return** is what you earn from every source: the **capital gain** (the price rises above what you paid) plus the **dividends** you received. The **dividend yield** is just the dividend part: annual dividend divided by price.

Over time, gains generate more gains. That is **compounding**: each year you earn on your original capital and on what you have already earned.

To compare different periods you use the **annualised return** or **CAGR** (compound annual growth rate): the constant yearly pace at which you would have grown. Formula: (final value / starting value) raised to 1/years, minus 1.

Finally, the **nominal** return is the one you see in euros. The **real** return subtracts **inflation**: how much your purchasing power grew.

## An example
You buy a share at €100. A year later it is worth €106 and you have received €3 in dividends.

- Capital gain: €6 (+6 %).
- Dividend: €3 (3 % on what you paid).
- Total return: (106 + 3 − 100) / 100 = **+9 %**.

Compounded: €1,000 at 7 % a year for 10 years is 1,000 × 1.07^10 ≈ **€1,967**, nearly double, not €1,700.

CAGR: your portfolio goes from €10,000 to €16,000 in 5 years. (16,000 / 10,000)^(1/5) − 1 ≈ **9.9 % a year**.

Real: if you earn 6 % nominal and inflation is 3 %, your real return is 1.06 / 1.03 − 1 ≈ **2.9 %**, not 3 %.

## The common mistake
Keeping an eye on the price and forgetting dividends. For stocks that pay a lot, the accumulated difference is huge.

Another one: dividing the total return by the number of years. +60 % in 5 years is not 12 % a year: it is 9.9 %, because growth compounds.

And looking only at the nominal figure. Earning 2 % while inflation is 3 % means losing purchasing power, even if the balance goes up.
