Two assets can both return 8 % a year and feel completely different: one climbs smoothly, the other puts you through 40 % falls. Two measures capture that difference.

## The idea
**Volatility** measures how much the price moves. Technically it is the **standard deviation** of returns: how far they stray, on average, from their mean. It is calculated from daily returns and annualised by multiplying by the square root of 252, the number of trading days in a year.

More volatility means a bumpier road, not necessarily a worse result.

The **maximum drawdown** is the worst fall from a peak to the lowest point after it. It answers "how much could I have lost if I had bought at the worst moment?". It is what you actually feel.

## An example
A fund has a daily volatility of 1 %. Annualised: 1 % × √252 ≈ 1 % × 15.9 = **15.9 %**. A typical year tends to land within that order of magnitude of its average.

Now the fall. Your €10,000 portfolio drops to €7,000: a 30 % fall. To get back to €10,000 you need 10,000 / 7,000 − 1 ≈ **+42.9 %**, not 30 %.

This asymmetry grows fast:

- −10 % needs +11.1 %.
- −20 % needs +25 %.
- −50 % needs **+100 %**: €100 that falls to €50 has to double.

## Volatility is not permanent loss
A fall is only a loss if you sell. If the business stays healthy, the price can recover, though sometimes it takes years. And sometimes it does not: a company that goes bankrupt does not come back. So volatility tells you what the journey is like, not where you end up.

## The common mistake
Treating volatility as the whole of risk. Real risk depends on your horizon and on whether you can hold on without selling. Someone who needs the money in a year cannot wait for a recovery; someone investing for 20 years can.

Another mistake: looking only at volatility and not at drawdown. A moderately volatile portfolio can still have a deep fall if everything drops together. Look at both, and ask yourself whether you could watch your portfolio fall 40 % without touching it.
