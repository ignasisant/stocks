A stock is a small slice of a company. Buy one and you become an owner, even if of a tiny part.

## The idea
Companies are split into thousands or millions of equal parts, called **shares**. Whoever holds them is a shareholder: the owner of that fraction, with a claim on its profits.

A company usually reaches the stock market through an **IPO** (initial public offering). From then on, its shares are bought and sold between investors at a price that changes every second.

There are two ways to make money:

- **Price gain**: you sell for more than you paid.
- **Dividends**: the company pays out part of its profits to shareholders. Not all of them do.

The price alone does not tell you how big a company is. The **market capitalisation** does: price per share × number of shares.

Two codes identify a stock. The **ticker** is short and depends on the exchange (the same stock can have different symbols in two markets). The **ISIN** is 12 characters and is unique worldwide for that security.

## An example
A company has 10 million shares and trades at €20.

- Market cap: 10,000,000 × €20 = **€200 million**.
- You buy 100 shares: 100 × €20 = **€2,000**. You own 0.001 % of the company (100 / 10,000,000).

A year later the price rises to €23 and the company pays a €0.50 dividend per share.

- Price gain: 100 × (€23 − €20) = **€300**.
- Dividend: 100 × €0.50 = **€50**.
- Total: €350 on €2,000, that is **+17.5 %** (before taxes and fees).

## The common mistake
Thinking a "cheap" €5 share is better than an "expensive" €500 one. The unit price means nothing on its own: it depends on how many shares exist. What counts is what the whole company is worth and what part you pay for.

Another mistake is looking only at the price gain. With dividends reinvested over many years, a large part of the total return comes from them.

And remember: a stock guarantees nothing. If the company does badly, the price falls and you can lose part or all of what you invested.
