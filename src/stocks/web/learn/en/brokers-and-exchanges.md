You do not buy shares "on the exchange": you buy them through a broker, which sends your order to the exchange. Knowing who does what tells you where your shares really are.

## The idea
The **exchange** (or market) is where buy and sell orders meet, such as BME, Xetra or Nasdaq. The **broker** is the intermediary you have an account with: it takes your order, sends it to the market and holds your securities.

That last job is called **custody**. Your shares are not the broker's: they are recorded in your name inside the firm, kept apart from its own money (segregation). If the broker fails, your securities are not part of its assets.

If something does go wrong, there is a safety net. In Spain, the **Fondo de Garantía de Inversiones (FOGAIN)** covers up to €100,000 per investor and firm when the firm cannot return your assets. Other countries have equivalent schemes, with different limits. It does not cover market losses.

When you buy, the trade is not completed instantly. **Settlement** is the moment money and securities actually change hands. In the US it is T+1 (one business day later) since May 2024. In Europe it is still T+2 (two business days).

Many brokers offer **fractional shares**: investing a fixed amount instead of a whole number of shares.

## An example
On Monday you buy one US stock and one European stock.

- The US one settles on **Tuesday** (T+1).
- The European one on **Wednesday** (T+2).

Even before settlement you see the holding in your portfolio, and its price moves.

With fractions: you want to invest €50 in a share that costs €200. You buy 50 / 200 = **0.25 shares**.

## The common mistake
Thinking FOGAIN protects you if the price falls. It only covers the firm's failure, not investment losses.

Another slip is looking only at the fee and forgetting who you trade with: where it is regulated, where it holds your securities and which guarantee scheme covers it. With a foreign broker, check which protection applies to you.
