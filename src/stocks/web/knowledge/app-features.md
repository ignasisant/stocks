The full description of every page and feature, one section per stop of the guided tour. The tour itself shows a two-line summary; this is the detail behind it.

## Welcome to TopStocks
TopStocks turns a broker statement into your real numbers: FIFO lots, P/L in your own currency at the ECB rate of each trade's own date, and what your tax office will want. This tour walks through each feature, says whether your account has it switched on, and takes you to it. Two minutes, and you can leave and come back at any point.

## Import your transactions
Drop a statement: every row is parsed and previewed before anything is written to your ledger. Revolut (CSV and PDF), Revolut crypto, Trading 212, Trade Republic (the Transaction export CSV), DEGIRO, Interactive Brokers, ClickTrade / Saxo and a generic CSV are recognised. **Any crypto exchange's export** works too — Binance, Coinbase, Kraken, Bitpanda, Crypto.com, Koinly or whichever wrote it: just drop the file, the assistant works out its columns, and coin-to-coin swaps, staking rewards and deposits are valued at that day's price. Re-importing the same statement never duplicates a row. A **holdings report** — what you own and at best the average paid, but no dates — is not refused either: each line asks the price you paid (the report's own average when it printed one) and offers the days that price was inside the session's low–high range, newest first; pick one or type a date, and the dated lines are booked as buys. A line left undated is left out, never guessed. If your browser or work device blocks the file picker, **paste the statement text** instead — same parse, same preview, same commit. No statement to hand yet? **Load an example statement** puts a real one through the same parse, preview and commit, and **Clear last import** takes it back out again. Not ready to hand over a real statement? **Use demo transactions** on the Portfolio page fills the ledger with a made-up book of seven well-known stocks so every tab has something in it; it is labelled as demo wherever it shows, and your first real import deletes it. Broker not listed? **Type your positions instead** (on Import and on the demo banner) opens the assistant with the start of a sentence in the box, like "I hold: 10 NVDA at 120, bought 2024-03-01"; it books what you type once you confirm, and that first trade removes the demo book like an import would. Shares moved between brokers are recognised even when the two statements name the company differently (an ISIN at one, the symbol at the other) or the arrival reads as a plain buy, and booking them as a move can be undone. Everything else in the app — positions, P/L, allocation, tax, dividends, fees — derives from this one ledger.

## Positions and custody
Your open lots with live prices, market value and weight in your reporting currency, plus which broker each holding actually sits in — a ticker split across two custodians shows as a split. Every ticker cell links through to its full page.

## Real return and risk
Two different questions, two sections. **Real return** is what your book actually did: time-weighted return (flow-adjusted, so a deposit never reads as a gain), money-weighted return (IRR), volatility and worst drawdown. Under it, **What moved your portfolio** is a waterfall for the same window: start value, net deposits, price moves, currency moves, dividends and fees, ending exactly on the end value, with the biggest gains and drags by ticker. **Risk of the current basket** is about what you hold today — allocation by sector, geography, currency and broker.

## Five-year projection
A range, not a prediction: 4,000 simulated scenarios from today's value, with **stocks and crypto moved separately** — each at its own real volatility and the correlation the two have shown. Stock growth is picked by **index reference** (MSCI World, S&P 500, Nasdaq-100) or, if you pick it, **your own holdings' rate** over the last two years — never by default; crypto starts at zero. Add a **monthly contribution** or a **target** and read the central case, the range and the chance of getting there, nominal or in today's money. With **a plan** saved, the tab opens on its contribution (a quarterly one spread per month), offers the plan's crypto share and spans your profile's horizon; type another monthly figure and **Use in my plan** stores it there, only when pressed.

## Realized gains and tax
Sales matched against your lots — FIFO, LIFO, average cost or UK S104, whichever your country uses — realized gains per year net of commissions, and your jurisdiction's own rules on top. Twelve are implemented (ES, US, UK, DE, FR, IT, IE, PT, CA, AU, AE, CH), each with its own brackets, allowances, reporting flags and reporting currency. Set your tax residence in Profile: it decides which rules run and which currency the ledger is replayed in.

## Dividends and broker costs
Dividends per year and per position, with withholding and the credit you can claim — and, beside them, what your shares were **entitled** to: every company's dividend history priced by the shares your ledger held the day before each ex-date, so a payment your statement never carried still shows up, and next year's income is estimated on today's holdings. On Home, those payments no statement carried join **Recent transactions** too, summed into one row marked as an estimate that opens onto each payment. The **Fees** tab then separates what the broker charged from what the spread cost you: each execution is measured against the midpoint of its own session, so the markup that never appears on a statement becomes a number.

## Sell or add
Every holding scored on **business quality** and on **price**, then weighed against the rest of the book: what to sell, what to trim because it has grown too big or too risky, and what to add to. Each move comes sized in money, with the **tax** a sale adds this year and the date a buy stops undoing a loss sold inside the repurchase window. Below, your watchlist's names and any ticker you type are judged **buy, watch or pass** on the same scale. Both tables show each name's **return** over one month, six months, a year or two years (pick the span beside the search; the link keeps it), in your own currency with dividends, and how many points it ran ahead of or behind the S&P 500 and its sector's ETF over the same days. A screen over public fundamentals, not advice. It needs a book of your own: until your first import (the example portfolio does not count) the page shows a blurred example of itself with an **Import portfolio** button.

## The daily briefing
One AI card on Home, written each evening after the US close and its after-hours session (20:15 New York time) for the next day, so it reads settled figures in the morning and stands through the day's sessions. Home shows its headline and first few lines, fired alerts first, and **Read Today** opens the whole card on the Today page: a headline naming the day's few things, then three parts by default. **Portfolio** sets your book's move today, this week and this month beside the S&P 500's, with the month drawn. **Today's alerts** are your own alerts the price crossed in the latest session — one that fired days ago is not on the card. A US company you hold that tells the SEC something worth stopping for — its past accounts can no longer be relied on, a new auditor, bankruptcy, debt called early, a listing rule broken, or an annual or quarterly report that will be late — is listed there too, once per filing, as a prompt to read it against why you hold it, not as a forecast. **Worth a look** has a line for each thing that came up: a print on a name you hold or watch in the next two weeks and how the latest ones did against the estimate, a **Fed or ECB** decision around the corner, the **tax calendar** (a filing deadline, the year closing with gains or losses still open, the next savings bracket, the day a sold loss can be bought back), positions at a 52-week edge and **the market your book sits in**. The **pencil** in the card's corner changes that: write what it should tell you each day, a line per thing, and each line becomes a section of its own, in your order, under your fired alerts — your biggest moves, the **events of the next seven days** (results, ex-dividend dates, the Fed and the ECB, tax deadlines), **insider trades** at your largest holdings (SEC Form 4, BaFin for German issuers, CNMV for Spanish ones), the last days' **headlines** naming your companies, the **8-Ks** they filed this week, which **big investors** added to or cut them last quarter (13F, dated), a review of your **exit criteria** against your alerts and notes, the main indices, rates or crypto, a long-term proposal marked as one. Start from a template or your own words; it reads back what it will fetch before the first card, and a line it has no data for says so rather than call the day quiet. The chat keeps the same list when you ask it for something every day. With **a plan** saved (Profile → My plan) it also measures your book against it, each such line linking to where you act on it — see Your plan. It **remembers what it told you**: a trigger it already showed stays off until its figure moves or its date gets closer. Each line opens its own **analysis**: the comparison it invites, made — the company against its peers, its sector and the S&P 500, its business figures and the analysts' consensus, what a sale saves in tax, which positions explain a month behind the index — as a verdict, a few points and the tables behind them, and every figure in the prose is checked against what was fetched. If no model answers it falls back to the same facts and tables without the prose. Home's cards are yours to order: **Customise** moves, hides and adds them, and the order follows your account to every device.

## Today
The day's page, written each morning for your book (Today in the menu, and in the mobile bar). Two layers. **Your part** is written when you open it, with the same daily card as Home and nothing more spent: your fired alerts, your book against the S&P 500, your routines, then three computed sections. **News** has the last days' Yahoo headlines naming your largest companies, their SEC 8-K filings this week and their latest results, each linked to its source; a headline is a quote, never advice. **Insiders** has open-market buys and sells by directors at your largest holdings over 30 days (SEC Form 4, BaFin for German issuers, CNMV for Spanish ones). **Comparisons** sets each position's last month, six months and year against its sector ETF and the S&P 500, in percentage points, with the Sell or add verdict (pick the span above the table); a fund or a coin, which has no sector, against the S&P 500 only. The AI adds at most one note per section, and only on a notable gap. **The market part** is the same for every account and written once a weekday morning before the open: the US 10-year yield, the 2-10 curve and the high-yield spread with their change over a month, the Fed and the ECB with their rate and the days to their next decision, US and euro-area inflation, the risk-appetite regime, how many sector ETFs hold their 100-session average, which sectors lead and trail the S&P 500 over the month, and crypto fear & greed with bitcoin dominance. Tabs filter the page (Alerts, Your book, News, Insiders, Comparisons, Macro, Trends, Routines), and the link keeps the tab. A source that did not answer is named under its section rather than the section being left out. Things already said stay off until they change: a headline is told once, an insider buy again only if the amount moves or a cluster appears. Signed out, the page shows the market part only.

## Your watchlist
The list every other page reads: Overview, Screener, Earnings and the ticker picker all run on it. Add a name by **searching** it — your own list, coins, funds, the SEC company map and every venue Yahoo quotes, so a symbol arrives with its real name instead of being spelled from memory. **Groups** are the app's grouping primitive: a ticker can sit in several, the list can be grouped by them, and Overview renders a section per group. Everything you edit here writes immediately. **Share** at the foot of the list sends the symbols on screen (the search and the group picked applied, up to 50) as a link, under a name you choose: only the symbols and that name go out, never positions, amounts or notes. Whoever opens it sees the list with each company's logo and name; **Follow all** puts them on their own watchlist as a group under that name (a guest signs in first and comes back to it).

## Market pulse
Where the market is heading, read against your own book — ranked by how much of it you actually need. The top card answers it in five seconds: **risk appetite** as a 0-100 score on its own meter, with a ghost pin where the score stood a month ago, and beside it the same regime restated as what it does to your basket. **Why** opens the number up: all eight inputs — trend, breadth, volatility, term structure, credit, stocks-versus-bonds, junk demand and emerging markets — each with its own bar, a tick at where it stood a month ago and its raw level, so the score is auditable rather than a black box; an input that could not be built is named and greyed rather than quietly dropped. Alongside it, four readings that only exist as trends: how many indices and sectors are above their own trend, the stock/bond correlation (positive means both legs fall together), and which of the four rates regimes the last quarter describes. **Your book against this regime** measures your basket's sensitivity to equities, rates, credit and the dollar — each pill is the three-month drift, which is the regime moving under you without your having traded. **The detail** keeps the long tables — indices, gauges, rates, inflation, rotation, factors and cross-asset — behind one tab strip, every row the same shape: the level, the change over 1w / 1m / 3m / 12m, a sparkline, and whether the series is in an uptrend or turning. **Positioning** adds the CFTC's weekly Commitments of Traders: speculators' net futures position in each market against its last three years, flagged **crowded** in the top or bottom 10 % (a one-sided trade, not a forecast). Every row there carries an **i** beside its name that says what the series is and how to read it, because “MOVE” and “2s10s” are not names that explain themselves.

## Any ticker, with its sources
Price and chart, from one day to its whole listed history, with a zoom range that reports the move over exactly the window you selected and a measure between any two dates (press, hold and slide, or two fingers, on a phone; a right-click on a computer), plus financials, estimates, what **analysts** say (their rating, price targets, which way they are revising earnings, and a year of named **rating changes** — upgrades, downgrades, initiations and target moves by firm, US listings only), the options market's read (at-the-money **implied volatility** and the **put/call** ratio by open interest and by volume), insider activity and news. Beside the price, a **Trend** figure says whether a share, a fund, a coin or an index runs up (above a rising 200-day average), down (below a falling one) or mixed, with its 12-1 momentum and its return over 12 months against the listing's home index (IBEX 35 for Madrid, DAX for Xetra, S&P 500 for the US) — a fund against the market its stocks trade in instead, so an emerging-markets ETF quoted in London reads against EEM, not the FTSE 100; a listing younger than about 11 months reads "short history", and bond, money-market and closed-end funds have no trend figure, since a cash fund's line only climbs. **Short selling** shows who is betting against the share: in the Netherlands, the UK and Germany the public registers name every holder of a net short of 0.5 % or more, with the total; for US listings, FINRA's daily short-sale volume and Yahoo's short interest (share of float, days to cover, change since the prior report). For European issuers, annual figures missing from Yahoo are filled from the audited report filed in ESEF (labelled with its source). Near the foot of the page, just above the KPI sources, **Compare** opens with the listing over the S&P 500, its sector's ETF and a few related companies; chips add more related names and the search anything else (the Nasdaq 100 or emerging markets included), up to six lines, each as its total return in your own currency since the first day of the window (one month to two years), so which did better is simply which line ends higher; a listing with no price history or no exchange rate into your currency is named under the chart rather than drawn in the wrong money. The related names offered first are the same industry's from the sector screen's nightly cohort, then Yahoo's "people also follow". **Comparables** lays the KPIs of the names you pick side by side, with medals for the best composite rank, a **peer median** column (the rivals' median and their middle half, P25 – P75, the company itself left out) and a • on any figure more than 3σ from the others' median. The **Fundamentals** grid reads each company on its own lens: a bank on P/TBV, ROTCE, dividend yield and payout instead of EV/EBITDA and ROIC; a REIT or a utility on yield, payout and EV/EBITDA, with net debt judged against its industry's line and a landlord's P/E and new shares shown without a verdict. Under each tile, **Peers** gives the rivals' median (the same industry from the nightly scan, else the sector), and when margins sit at a peak the P/E also shows what it would be at a normal margin. A multiple at zero or below gets no verdict and a negative P/E reads **Loss**. When one-offs (gains on stakes, write-downs) move the last four quarters' profit by more than a quarter, the P/E (TTM) is on normalized income and the tile shows the reported one beside it. Above the grid, **As a new buy** gives the verdict **Sell or add** would give the stock as a new position (buy, watch, pass or bet), its quality and price scores and the reasons, with a link to open it there. Under the grid, **Watch** lists the review engine's cautions for the name (peak margin, heavy dilution, earnings not backed by cash…). The header names what the symbol is — stock, equity fund, bond fund, money market, closed-end fund, crypto or index — and the page shows the figures that kind is read by: a money-market fund shows its yield against the central bank's rate, not an RSI. Every KPI carries where it came from: **Fact** is verifiable in a filing, **Consensus** is analyst estimates, **Derived** is computed here. The search box at the top of the page works from anywhere in the app and labels each result with its kind. **Analyze with AI** in the header hands the whole company to the assistant — one click asks it about the business, the fundamentals, the valuation against its own history and how the position fits your book (funds and coins get their own question; an index, which nobody holds directly, gets none). The question is short, but the assistant is handed a brief written for that company: the lens for its industry (a uranium enricher read as nuclear fuel, not oil and gas; a biotech by its pipeline and cash runway), whether you hold it and at what weight, a report just out or coming up, the shape the answer should take, and a few notes on the business drafted once and kept a month. For a US company the brief also names its largest institutional holders from the SEC's quarterly 13F filings — each manager's stake and whether it opened, added, cut or sold out over the quarter — with Yahoo's percentages for whatever those lack. The arrow beside it asks for one angle instead — the **bear case** (how it fails and when to sell), the **latest results** against consensus, or how it **fits your portfolio** — each in a new conversation. **Share** beside it sends the page itself as a link, whose preview card shows the company's name and symbol, never a price or anything of your book. A coin's page is read as a coin: how far under its **all-time high** it trades, where it sits in its **cycle** (Fear & Greed, the Mayer multiple, the 200-week average, the halving clock), what perpetual-swap traders pay to hold it (**funding** and **open interest**), bitcoin's and ether's **implied volatility** (Deribit's DVOL, against its past year), the total **stablecoin** supply, and for a chain or protocol its **value locked** and the fees its users paid over 30 days (DefiLlama) and, when you hold it, its share of your crypto and what selling it at a loss would save in tax.

- **Cash-flow value (DCF)**: free cash flow to the firm (operating cash flow less capex, plus after-tax interest) grown five years at the trailing FCF growth (kept between 0 and 15 %), a Gordon terminal value, discounted at a WACC (default 9 %, terminal growth 2.5 %), less net debt, per diluted share, against the price, with a WACC 7-11 % by terminal growth 1.5-3.5 % table. Growth, WACC and terminal growth are editable on the card. Shown only for companies with positive trailing FCF and not for banks, insurers, REITs or utilities. An illustration of the assumptions, not a target or advice.
- **Reconstructed P/E**: split-adjusted close over trailing-12-month diluted GAAP EPS rebuilt from quarterly filings, stepped forward on each filing date (each day uses only figures already public); loss-making stretches are omitted. It can differ from the P/E (TTM) in Fundamentals when the latest quarter is in Yahoo's trailing EPS but its 10-Q/10-K is not on SEC EDGAR yet, or because of adjusted vs GAAP EPS.
- **Annual chart**: hatched bars are consensus revenue, the dashed line consensus EPS with the low-high analyst range; years beyond published estimates are extrapolated (revenue at next-FY consensus growth, EPS at long-term growth).
- **German insiders (BaFin, MAR art. 19)**: officers, directors and close associates trading their own company's stock, notified within 3 business days, last 12 months. BaFin gives an average price and an aggregate euro volume, so the share count is derived (volume / price).
- **Spanish insiders (CNMV, MAR art. 19)**: the same disclosure for Spanish issuers (.MC listings), last 12 months. The CNMV publishes a form per notification with the share count and unit price; venues of the same day are merged, and an amended form replaces the one it corrects. Older notifications fill in over the first few page loads.
- **Closed-end funds**: a fixed number of shares trading above or below NAV. Figures come from the fund's SEC filings and Yahoo's NAV line; the expense ratio includes interest on leverage, and part of the distribution can be return of capital (Section 19 notices). No statements, insiders, earnings dates or comparables.
- **ETFs and index funds**: the page opens on five years (an index too). The fund card says what the wrapper is, read off its name and ISIN: accumulating or distributing, domicile, UCITS, the index it tracks, a currency hedge, and synthetic only when the name says swap (physical is never claimed, since nobody publishes it). It adds what the domicile withholds on US dividends (15 % in an Irish fund, 30 % in a Luxembourg, German or French one; a US fund has no KID for EU retail), which currency actually moves it, the average value traded a day, a warning under 100 M of assets (the size issuers close), and when to trade: the hours its stocks' market is open too (tightest spreads), or that it is shut all session (an Asian basket quoted in Europe: use limit orders). Signed in, **Fit with your portfolio** sets it against your book: its top holdings you already own and what they weigh in it, your sectors beside the fund's (and without it, when held) with any over your plan's cap in red, and its TER beside the spread and commission your own fills of it paid. Tracking difference is not published, so it is not counted. A Spanish resident is reminded an ETF cannot be switched tax-free (traspaso) the way an index fund can.

## Sector screen and earnings calendar
Pick a sector and see a comparable cohort of companies — the sector ETF's basket, widened with listed names outside the US — ranked on valuation and quality, with the best three called out and read for you. A second **best three** ranks the same cohort on technical momentum instead — trend, RSI, MACD, distance from its 52-week high, 3-month return and volume. Under both, the cohort sits on the **quality against price** map from Sell or add — same scores and bars, each name coloured by its verdict as a new buy, your holdings filled and sized by weight. The podium ranks the cohort against itself, so its winner can still land in the dear half; the map judges against fixed bars. A link carries the sector's buys you don't own into Sell or add to weigh them against your book. Then see which of your holdings report next, beside the **tax deadlines** of your tax residence — flagged 30 days ahead — and the **ex-dividend dates** of what you hold, each with what it pays on your shares; last year's schedule fills the months nobody has declared yet. The same calendar marks the day a position sold at a loss can be **bought back** without deferring the loss, and the **Fed and ECB** rate decisions. Click any date and it says what it means for you — on a dividend, the cash that lands, net of the withholding your own statements show.

## Learn
Short lessons that teach investing from the start, grouped into **paths** you read in order: **Getting started** (what a stock and a fund are, how a broker and an order work, costs, market hours), **Return and risk** (total return, time-weighted return against IRR, volatility and drawdown, VaR, beta and benchmarks, currency risk) and **Tax in Spain** (the savings base and its brackets, FIFO, the two-month rule, offsetting losses, dividends and foreign withholding, Modelo 720/721, the tax calendar). Each lesson takes three to five minutes and ends with where the idea shows up in the app (**See it in the app**), the terms it uses and the way on to the next one. Mark a lesson read and its path's bar fills; signed in, the ticks follow your account, and without signing in they stay in that browser. The **Glossary** tab defines every term the lessons use, searchable by name, by another name it goes by ("PER" finds P/E) or by its definition, accents and case ignored; each term links to the lesson that teaches it. The lessons are general teaching, the same for everyone: they never read your book, and the tax lessons say the date they were last checked and that rules change every year.

## The assistant
The chat panel on the right, reachable from every page, with your real book in hand: it reads your positions, live quotes, a company's own page (its fundamentals against peers, P/E against its history, the DCF and its grid, the analysts' consensus, revisions and target changes), filings and the web, and picks the analysis skill that fits the question. A tax question gets the **Tax engine & lot methods** skill: it prices a sale or a loss harvest through the same engine as the Realized & tax tab, explains the lot method and repurchase rule of your tax residence, and always says it is an estimate, not tax advice. While it researches, each lookup is listed with what it is for; a longer research also shows its **plan**, the steps it means to take ticked off as it goes, and says what it could not find rather than filling the gap from memory. A question that needs more — or the **In depth** switch in the lens menu, for the next question only — gets a longer research that reads more sources and can take a couple of minutes; on the shared chain it counts as two messages. An empty thread opens with a few suggestions built from your own watchlist, so it has something useful to say before you have imported anything — and, until you have, a first row that takes you to **Import**. Without signing in you see the assistant but cannot use it: it opens on a sample answer about the example portfolio and a sign-in button, and a question you asked from a page is waiting in the box once you are signed in. It is there for investing, markets, your portfolio and the app: on the shared chain a question about anything else may get one line saying so instead of an answer. It runs keyless on the shared TopStocks chain within a daily cap; connect your own provider key for no cap and any model that provider offers, or **sign in with OpenRouter** and have one minted on your account without copying anything. You can ask out loud as well: press the microphone on the composer and what you say is written into the field as you say it, then checked against the recording when you stop, so you read and fix it before it goes — the audio itself is never stored. While an answer is being written the send button becomes a stop button: press it and the answer stops where it is, keeping whatever it had already written, and any lookup still running stops with it. A connection that drops mid-answer is not read as a stop: the question is answered again the next time you open the app. If the app itself restarts mid-answer, reopening the conversation picks the answer back up from where its research had got to, without looking anything up twice or counting the question again. You can also move to another conversation, or start a new one, while an answer is still being written: it keeps being written, its row in the conversation list says **Answering…**, and when it is done a notice with **Open** takes you back to it, a dot marks the conversation until you open it (and the assistant's button while the panel is shut), and a tab in the background shows the count in its title. On a computer, with the panel dragged wide by its left edge, the conversations you have open sit as **tabs** under its header — up to five, each one press away, with the same dots for one still answering or answered; closing a tab keeps the conversation in the list. A statement dropped or pasted into the chat — the file itself, rows copied from a spreadsheet, or a PDF as base64 — is previewed under the conversation: first how many rows are ready, how many carry a warning, how many are already in your book and how many were rejected, then the rows themselves. On a phone each row is a tick box, the trade and its amount, and a row with a warning has a **!** that explains it and lets you fix its currency on the spot. On a computer **Open wide** turns the whole chat panel into the review — a tab for each kind of row, a summary of the operations, tickers, dates and net amount per currency beside them — and **Back to the drawer** (or Esc) returns it to its old width. An answer with a wide table offers **See wide** under it. When a question fails, the notice says why in one line and offers at most two ways on, such as retrying, trying another model or adding your own key. At the message limit the notice counts down and asks again by itself. Asked to change something — a favourite, an alert, a position — it shows what it understood and waits for **Confirm**, with **Edit** to fix a misheard price or symbol first. It may also offer one change of its own — an alert at a price it just found, a ticker for your watchlist — as a card under its answer that does nothing until you approve it. It can **fix your transactions** too — list, edit, delete or add rows, book a company two brokers spelled differently under one ticker, turn a sale and a buy into the **transfer** they were, or check the whole book for mistakes like these: each change is shown row by row with what it does to your holdings and realized gains before you confirm, and has an **Undo** after. A question that asks for a decision ("should I buy…?") is argued first by a **bull and a bear analyst**, and the answer weighs the two instead of taking the side the question leaned to. Ask for a chart — a share, a currency pair, an index, gold — and the app draws a **price chart** from real closes under the answer, with chips to switch the window, rather than the model sketching one from memory. Ask whether a name is in a **trend**, or when to get in or out, and it reads the daily closes: price against its 200-day average and whether that average is rising, 12-1 momentum, distance from the 52-week high and low, the return against the listing's home index (IBEX 35 for Madrid, DAX for Xetra, S&P 500 for the US) over 6 and 12 months, with RSI and ATR as context — and quotes only those levels, as timing on top of the fundamentals, never as the reason to buy. The Claude connector has the same read. Ask how you are doing against the S&P and **your own portfolio** is one of its lines, as its time-weighted return; ask how your book is split and it draws a **donut** by position, sector, country or currency, funds looked through. Asked which losses would offset this year's gains, it replays each through your tax rules and lists the **harvest candidates** with what each saves and the day a repurchase is clear; asked to take a position to a weight, it sizes the trade on a **target-weight slider** and prices the tax on the sale. It **remembers what you tell it about yourself** — say "remember that…", or mention a goal or a preference in passing — and each memory it saves, corrects or drops is shown under the answer with **Undo**; name something you discussed in an earlier conversation and that conversation is brought into the answer, with a link back to it. The list lives in **Settings → Memory** (and on your Profile), where you can edit it or switch it off. A conversation nobody has written in for 30 days is removed, and the assistant stops recalling it; what you asked it to remember stays. A memory can hold until a day — say "until the end of the year", or set the date when you edit it — and once that day has passed it stays on the list, dimmed, but no longer shapes answers. Saying a rule again with other figures ("no more than 5 % in one position" after "10 %") corrects the one kept instead of keeping both, and a new memory that may clash with an older one names it under the answer with **Replace it**. Each memory shows how often your questions touched it; when the list is full, the chat and the screen name the one used least as the one to let go. Once a week the assistant reads the list against what you have said since and may propose a tidy-up — drop what ran out or went unused, merge two that say the same, reword one you changed your mind about — shown at the top of **Settings → Memory** with **Apply** and **Dismiss**; nothing changes until you apply it. An earlier conversation is found under any name a holding on your watchlist goes by: ask about "Nvidia" and the one where you wrote "NVDA" comes back too. Any answer can be **shared** by link: the share button under it shows a preview of exactly what the link will show — with **Hide my amounts** on by default, so money and share counts read as •••, while tickers, percentages and dates stay; tables of amounts and anything you could press are left out — then **Create link** gives a link to copy or send to WhatsApp, X, LinkedIn, Telegram, Facebook or email (or the phone's own share sheet). The link is a frozen copy: it keeps working if the conversation is removed, and **Stop sharing** takes it down. Whoever opens it lands in TopStocks with the chat showing that answer; signed in, **Keep asking** starts a conversation of their own from it, and a guest is offered the sign-in and comes back to the same answer.

## Decision journal
A record of what the app proposed, written by the app (with one exception, below), and nothing in it asks why you bought anything. Every move it actually **showed** you lands here — a sale, trim or harvest on the daily card, a verdict on **Sell or add**, an alert or a watchlist change offered in the chat — with the reasons given, the figures it was made on (quality and price scores, weight, P&L, the loss to harvest, the alert level) and what came of it: **open** while it is still being proposed, **followed** or **declined** when you answered it in the chat — or **followed** on its own when a sale or buy of that name turns up in your book within 30 days of it being shown, nothing asked — **lapsed** when the page stopped proposing it or 30 days went by without it being shown again. A proposal shown again is the same entry, counted once a day; a different move on the same thing (a trim that became a sell) replaces it and the two are linked. Read it whole in **Profile → Journal**, filtered by state, by where it was proposed and by ticker; on a ticker's page a **Journal** chip in the header opens that name's entries in a side panel. A month and again a quarter after a sale, trim, add or buy was first shown, the app reads the price back and keeps the move since, whatever you did with it, so you can see whether the name went the way the move bet on. The tab opens with the track record (how many moves were proposed, taken, turned down, lapsed, and how many of those priced went their way) and a **Download Markdown** link: the whole journal as one file a notes app such as Obsidian reads, with tags and links between entries. The daily card, the assistant and the Claude connector read it too, so a move you declined is not pressed again in the same words.

**Exit rules** (kill criteria) are the one part that is yours: per holding, the line at which you said you would sell, decided with a clear head before the price tests it. Three families. Price rules: a close at or below a price (in the listing's currency), a loss on cost, a fall from the high of the last year, and a weight in the book. Fundamental rules: revenue growth (on the same quarter a year before), the gross margin or the operating margin falling under a percent, for 1 to 4 quarters running; they are read from the company's quarterly filings once a new quarter is due, so they move a few times a year. Event rules: a CEO change, a guidance cut or a dividend cut; each morning a model reads the name's new 8-K filings and headlines since the rule was set and marks it met only with a passage that says so, which the card and the journal quote with where it was read. An event rule met is a lead to check, not a confirmed fact. Ask the assistant to "help me write exit rules for NVDA", or press **Write them with the assistant** in the ticker's journal panel: it drafts them with your thesis (why you hold it, in your words) as a card you approve or edit, and nothing is saved until you do. A new rule of the same kind replaces the old one, and both stay linked. The daily card checks them every morning: a rule met heads the card ("NVDA hit your exit rule") and stays **met** until you retire it; one within 3 % or 3 points of its line shows as near, at most once a week. Claude, connected with edits allowed, can write and retire them the same way, once you agree to the draft. The assistant reads them before any answer about selling or holding that name. Retire one from the ticker's journal panel; it stays in the journal as **retired**. Thesis rules check the business itself: one to three pillars you hold the company for ("hyperscalers keep raising AI capex"), not a price. Every day a model reads the name's new filings and business headlines against each pillar and sorts what it finds: noise (price moves, ratings, opinion, good news — most of it), a fact to watch, or one that damages the pillar. The rule is met on damage, or when the same pillar draws a watch again within 30 days, with the quote and the pillar on the card, the journal and a notification. A busy book is read in turns, ten names a day at most. For now Claude, connected with edits allowed, writes them; the thesis text beside any rule is kept as you wrote it.

**Buy rules** are the other side, per name, held or not: a close at or below a price, a fall of some percent from the high of the last year, a forward P/E at or under a figure, or revenue growth, gross margin or operating margin above a percent for 1 to 4 quarters. Ask the assistant ("buy NVDA under 120") or have Claude write them; the name joins your watchlist so its closes are followed. The daily card says when one is met ("NVDA is in your buy zone") or near, as a level you set, never as advice to buy; they sit in the journal panel under their own heading, and are retired the same way.

## Notifications
One summary a day and no more: the **daily AI summary** (the day's actions on your book — the same card as Home), by default 08:30 Monday to Friday. You choose when it arrives: **at a time** of your own (kept in your browser's time zone) or **after the US close**, pushed as soon as that evening's card is written (about 22:30 in Madrid). Switching to the evening never sends a second one the same day. Besides it, price alerts during market hours and a reminder after a quiet week, all as push notifications on each device you switch them on for; a finished answer too. The evening "day's numbers" digest and the Sunday weekly review were retired in November 2026 — the week still comes whole in the Friday email. Each summary is also kept as a conversation of its own under **Scheduled**, a folding block at the top of the chat's conversation history, and tapping its notification opens that conversation, so what the notification folded away is never lost. A run missed while the server was down catches up within six hours, never twice the same day. The jobs run server-side, so nothing depends on you having TopStocks open. To turn it off or move it: the switch on the summary's row in **Profile → Notifications**, and **Change time** right below it (time or after the close, and the weekdays); the notification itself carries a **Change time** button that opens the same editor. **See past ones** opens the Scheduled block.

The same tab has a **This device** card that switches notifications on for the browser or phone you are using: it is per device (switch it on in each browser you use), and the toggles apply to all of them. **Send test notification** checks it works. A finished answer is sent only while the app is not on screen, and tapping it opens that conversation; the chat also offers it once under an answer that is taking a while. On iOS (16.4 or later) it only works once the site is added to the Home Screen; in a Safari tab the card says so. Only on deployments with a push key.

One message is not a notification: the **Friday email**, sent after the US close to the address you sign in with, to every account that imported a portfolio — no device needed. It carries the week in your reporting currency: portfolio value and the week's change with money added or withdrawn kept out of the gain, your return against the S&P 500 bought with the same money on the same days from your first day (then week, month and year to date), the names that moved the book, the week's trades, realized gains, dividends and fees, exit rules that fired, earnings and ex-dividend dates of what you hold next week, and concentration. Names with no price are listed as counted at cost. A last section lists what the account has not set up yet (records to fix, a plan, exit rules, notifications, tax residence…) with a link to each. The **Email** card in the Notifications tab switches it off, and every email has a one-click unsubscribe link.

## Your portfolio in other AI apps (MCP)
Profile's **MCP** tab connects TopStocks to AI apps that speak MCP, so you can ask about your book from there with your real figures and what the assistant remembers about you. Copy the **connector address**, pick your app and follow its steps:
- **Claude** (claude.ai, Desktop, mobile): Settings → Connectors → Add custom connector, paste the address. Every Claude plan can add one, the free one too (one connector there); on Team and Enterprise the organisation's owner adds it.
- **Claude Code**: `claude mcp add --transport http topstocks <address>`.
- **ChatGPT**: Settings → Apps → Advanced settings → Developer mode on, then Create app with the address and OAuth (plans that offer developer mode).
- **Codex**: `codex mcp add topstocks --url <address>`, then `codex mcp login topstocks`.
- **Gemini CLI**: `gemini mcp add --transport http topstocks <address>`, then `/mcp auth topstocks` inside Gemini. The Gemini app itself takes no MCP.
- **Cursor**, **VS Code** (Copilot agent mode) and **Windsurf**: the tab shows the config entry or command to add.
- **Le Chat** (Mistral): Connectors → Add connector → Custom MCP connector.
- **Any other** MCP app: add the address as a remote (HTTP) server; one that only runs local servers can use `npx -y mcp-remote <address>`.

Each app then opens a TopStocks screen to approve it. It only reads unless you also tick editing when you approve, and every change it makes is shown first and can be undone; it can never empty your book or delete your account. The tab lists every app connected, with a **Revoke** beside each — signing out of TopStocks does not disconnect them. Only on deployments that serve the connector.

## Your investor profile
Risk tolerance, horizon, focus and constraints. The assistant reads it before answering, so the analysis is framed for your mandate instead of a generic one. The **focus** areas do one more thing: the watchlist tab then offers example tickers from them that you do not already follow — examples to look at, never recommendations, and adding them removes nothing you have.

## Your plan
Profile → **My plan** holds the rules you want your book held to, in four optional blocks: a **target mix** by asset type (shares, equity funds, bond funds, money market, crypto) with an allowed drift in points; **risk caps** for one position, one sector and speculative holdings; a **regular contribution** (amount, monthly or quarterly, day of the month); and a **cash reserve** range. A blank field keeps the app's own line (40 % per sector, 3 % speculative), and the mix only saves once it adds up to 100 %. The tab shows today's mix against each target.

A fifth block, **Cash**, holds the money the app cannot see at your broker or bank: **cash to invest** counts in the money-market bucket and the reserve, and Sell or add can spend it; the **emergency cushion** is never counted anywhere. An optional interest rate goes with it. Neither enters your returns or your book's value. Both are figures you type, so the tab dates them and, after 60 days, asks you to bring them up to date — and so does the daily briefing, once.

Under the contribution, the tab says where it leads at your profile's horizon — the projection's central case and the range of eight scenarios in ten — with a link to the Projection tab.

On Home, the start card has a **Plan** step after the import. It is optional and does not count toward the card's progress; it opens the assistant's plan builder, and turns done once a plan is saved.

You can also ask the assistant to build one ("make me an investment plan"): it draws a card that asks only what the app does not already know — your risk and horizon when the profile was never saved, whether you keep an emergency cushion outside the broker, how much you add and how often, and, when your book does not say it, whether you prefer funds or single companies and how much crypto. The draft beside it is worked out by one fixed rule from those answers (equity from risk and capped by horizon, cash for a missing cushion, crypto at most a third of equity, caps by risk), redrawn on every tap, and **Save as my plan** stores it here — plus the profile, if it was blank. The emergency-fund question is about savings kept apart for months of spending, never invested: **I have none** keeps 10 % of the book in cash as that cushion, **under 6 months** 5 %, and **6 months or more** none, since the fund outside already covers it. The crypto answer is the slice of equity in coins, larger with risk and never above a third of it; the style answer is how much of equity sits in single companies rather than funds (about a fifth, half, or four fifths). It is a starting point from your own answers, not advice, and names no securities.

Sell or add obeys it: your caps replace the page's lines, a bucket past its target plus the drift gets a note (when it is shares, no new company is bought and new money waits), one under it gets a note too, and the "if you add money" list is sized to your next contribution with its date, plus any cash to invest you stated. The cash reserve is measured on money-market funds plus that stated cash. The assistant and the Claude connector read the plan before advising. Without a plan everything works as before.

The daily briefing measures your book against it too: a bucket outside its target and drift, a company or a sector past your cap, cash outside the reserve range and a contribution due within three days (with the buckets under target it could go to — never a security) each get a line, and the heaviest company or coin with neither an exit rule nor an alert gets one asking for it. A drift is never answered with a sale. Each line links to the page where you act on it — your plan, Sell or add, the tax tab, the calendar, the journal. Without a plan the card says once that one is missing. Its drawdown line follows your investor profile's risk either way (a position down 15 % for a conservative profile, up to 35 % for a very aggressive one; 25 % with no profile).

Under the plan, **Entry criteria** are the lines every buy or add is held to, whatever the name: revenue growth, operating margin, free-cash-flow yield and ROIC at least a percent; forward P/E and net debt over EBITDA at most a multiple; share dilution at most a percent a year; a dividend yield at least a percent; and never adding past a weight in the book. A blank field is no line. A note of principles in your own words ("no pre-revenue biotech") is read by the assistant before it suggests a buy; nothing measures it. Sell or add keeps its own verdict and, under a buy or an add, lists the lines it breaks; a company's page shows how many of them it meets beside the fundamentals. A figure the data does not give is neither met nor broken. The assistant and the Claude connector read them too, and say which a suggestion breaks. **Propose with AI** on that card, **My entry criteria** under a share's **Analyse with AI** menu, or asking the chat "propose my entry criteria" drafts the lines from your investor profile's risk, a dividend focus and your plan's position cap into fields you edit, blank or keep; **Save my criteria** stores them, replacing the ones saved, and your note is kept as you wrote it.

## Preferences
Reporting currency, language (English, Spanish or Italian), tax residence and the bracket inputs your jurisdiction reads, grouped into three cards; the tax card states what the choice decides (cost currency, share matching, tax year) instead of describing it. Nothing here has a Save button — every change is written as you make it. It is also where the ledger exports to CSV, where an account deletes itself, where **Signed-in devices** lists the browsers signed in (device and last activity) with a sign-out for each and one for all the others, and where this tour lives whenever you want it again. **Shared links** lists every answer and list you shared by link, each with **Stop sharing**; **Invite a friend** sends a link to the app itself, which shows nothing of your portfolio.
